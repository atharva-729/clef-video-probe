The aim

Can a model watch a live broadcast and tell us what is on screen?

Short answer: yesfor anything printed or laid out on screen
Short answer: not yetfor what is physically happening in the picture
  • QuestionGiven only the picture, can a model report what is happening on screen, moment by moment, reliably enough to drive something a viewer sees live?
  • Test5½ minutes of the James Webb launch broadcast, one frame every 5 seconds.
  • MethodCloudflare's Clef answers the same 19 multiple-choice questions on every frame, with a confidence for each answer.
  • Check1,251 answers scored against a person's labels. When an answer flips and stays flipped, we call it a launch event.

02 Events and detection

4 of 5 launch events caught from the answers alone. One fired 73 seconds early.

No event detector was trained. An event fires when one of Clef's answers changes and holds for two frames. A hit is within 10 seconds of the real moment.

The lateness is mostly the sampling. Looking at one frame per second instead of every five, ignition, the 100 km line and fairing jettison were each caught in the exact second.

04 How it was done

Sample, ask, check, detect.

Clef is not a chatbot. It takes a picture and a list of multiple-choice questions and returns a probability for every option in one pass, so there is no prose to parse and every answer carries a confidence.

Sample66 frames from a 330-second clip, one every 5 seconds.
AskThe same 19 questions per frame. Each has an escape option such as "can't tell".
CheckScore against labels drafted by a model, then reviewed and corrected by a person.
DetectA new answer at 60% confidence or more, held for two frames, becomes an event.

Does its confidence mean anything?

Group every answer by how confident Clef was, then check how often each group was right. The top group is right 99% of the time. That gives a workable rule: act on the confident answers automatically and send the rest to a person. The booster errors are the exception: they sit at 80–95% confidence.

05 Caveats

Read the numbers as an indication, not a benchmark.

One clip, one labeller.

One broadcast, 66 frames, labels checked by one person. A single frame moves any question by 1.5 points.

The booster error would not move.

Rewording the question, sampling every second and giving the model its previous answers all left it in place. It is a model error, not a setup error.

Editorial judgement is unproven.

"Is this a highlight?" and "is it safe to cut away?" beat the commonest answer by about 6 points. Not enough to automate an editor's call.

Liftoff is called late.

Clef keeps saying "on the pad" until the rocket is well clear, so liftoff is 9 to 10 seconds late even at one frame per second.

Speed was measured once.

About 1.5 seconds per frame, on one afternoon, through one provider.

The footage is not ours.

The broadcast includes partner graphics. Anything client-facing should use footage the company owns.

Wording matters more than expected
CLEF ANSWERED ABOUT THIS INSET

Late in the clip the rocket is not in the main picture, but Clef kept answering "in flight, boosters separated". It was describing the small animation in the corner. We had never told it to ignore the insets, so that failure was our question, not the model.

Rewording fixed it and broke a different question, which took one more rewording to recover. Every new use needs its questions tuned against labelled frames first.

06 What we tried and dropped

Cheaper, smarter, denser. Only denser paid off.

Layout questions only

The small model, Clef-Flash.

A sixth of the price, eight points worse, and no faster.

Overall accuracy88.6% → 80.9%
Editorial questions87% → 51%
Cost for the clip$0.046 → $0.007
Dropped

Telling it its previous answer.

No gain, and slightly worse exactly where the picture had just changed.

When the truth just changed76% → 70%

Keep for key moments

One frame per second.

Timing to the second, at five times the cost. Boosters still missed.

Replaced

Spotting ignition from the clock.

The broadcast hides the countdown from 0:50 to 2:10, so this fired 69 seconds late. "A flame appears" works.

Avoided

Forcing a guess.

Every question has a "can't tell" or "not visible" option, so the model can decline instead of inventing an answer.

Not run

07 What comes next

Run it on footage we own, on the questions it is good at.

Next stepThe same pipeline on a clip the company owns, in the domain of most commercial interest, with questions limited to what is printed or laid out on screen.
Live sports overlaysLooks feasible

Reading a scoreboard or on-screen graphic is the proven strength. Detecting the action itself, a wicket or a goal, is the booster question again and should be assumed weak until tested.

Video archivesLooks feasible

Knowing what kind of shot is on screen supports automatic chaptering and "find every clip showing…" search.

Content complianceWorth a trial

Auto-accept the 59% of answers given at high confidence and review the rest by hand.

Highlights and ad breaksNot ready

The editorial questions barely beat the commonest answer.

Cost is not the obstacle: all six runs behind this page, 519 model calls, came to 32 cents.

Method. Model: Cloudflare Clef via the OpenRouter Decisions API, question schema version 4. Frames sampled every 5 s from a 330 s clip. Answers are the highest-probability option; confidence is that probability. An event fires when a new answer holds for two consecutive frames at 60% confidence or more. Comparisons used Clef-Flash, one-per-second sampling around each event, and previous-frame context.

Video. Complete Webb Telescope Launch Broadcast, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center Scientific Visualization Studio, svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/14060. A joint NASA/ESA production with on-screen graphics from partners including CNES. Shown here for research; not endorsed by NASA, ESA or CNES.
