01 The question

Can a model tell us what is on screen, frame by frame, well enough to act on?

Clef is not a chatbot. You hand it a picture and a list of multiple-choice questions, and it hands back a probability for every option of every question in a single pass. No prose to parse, and a confidence number attached to each answer.

A launch broadcast is a hard, checkable test. One frame can hold live camera footage or an animation, a telemetry panel in French, a trajectory chart, a map and a control-room inset. Some of it is printed on the screen. Some of it you have to understand.

Sample. 66 frames from a 330-second clip, one every 5 seconds.
Ask. The same 19 questions about every frame. Each has an escape option such as "can't tell".
Check. 1,251 answers scored against labels made by a person.
Detect. When an answer flips and stays flipped, call it an event: ignition, liftoff, separation.

05 Where it broke
73 s early

It cannot see what the rocket is doing.

The moment the broadcast cuts to the animation, Clef decides the side boosters have separated, and says so on sixteen frames in a row. So the "booster separation" event fires at 2:10. It really happens at 3:23.

Nothing we tried moved it: rewording the question, sampling every second, and feeding the model its own previous answers.

06 The inset trap

Half of one failure was our question, not the model.

Late in the clip the telescope's foil fills the screen and the rocket is nowhere in the main picture. Our labels said "not visible". Clef kept answering "in flight, boosters separated, fairing gone".

It was looking at the small animation in the bottom-right corner, where all of that is true. We never told it to ignore the insets.

So we reworded the six vehicle questions to say "looking only at the main area of the screen". That fixed the inset. It also broke a different question, which took one more rewording to recover.

Every new use needs its questions tuned against labelled frames before the answers can be trusted.

07 The useful surprise

It mostly knows when it doesn't know.

Group every answer by how confident Clef was and check how often each group was right. When it says 90% or more, it is right 99% of the time, and that covers 59% of all its answers.

Below 60% confidence it is right less than half the time. That gives a rule you can run a product on: act on the confident answers automatically and send the rest to a person.

The booster streak is the exception that matters. Those wrong answers sit in the 80–95% range, which is why they are the story.

08 From answers to events

Four launch events caught. One fired a minute early.

Hollow rings are when each event really happened. Filled circles are when the detector called it, using nothing but changes in Clef's answers. The shaded zone is the ±10 seconds we count as a hit.

The clock that wasn't there.

Our first rule for ignition watched the countdown reach zero. The broadcast hides the clock from 0:50 to 2:10, so it fired 69 seconds late. Switching to "a flame appears" fixed it.

Sampling every second nails the timing.

Ignition, the 100 km line and fairing jettison are each caught in the exact second they happen. At 5-second sampling the delay is mostly the sampling.

The small model gets the boosters.

Clef-Flash calls booster separation 2 seconds after it happens, which the big model never manages. Then it loses the fairing 73 seconds early instead.

10 The bill, and the verdict

Everything on this page cost thirty-two cents.

Six runs, 519 model calls. One pass over the clip is under 5 cents, which works out at about 50 cents per hour of video. Cost is not what stands between this and a product. Knowing which questions to trust is.

Live sports overlaysLooks feasible

Reading a scoreboard or an on-screen graphic is the proven strength. Detecting the action itself, a wicket or a goal, is the booster question in another costume and should be assumed weak until tested.

Video archivesLooks feasible

Knowing what kind of shot is on screen supports automatic chaptering and "find every clip showing…" search.

Highlights and ad breaksNot ready

"Is this a highlight?" and "is it safe to cut away?" beat the commonest answer by about six points. That is not enough to automate an editor's call.

Content complianceWorth a trial

The confidence result fits: auto-accept the 59% of answers given at high confidence and review the rest by hand.

Read this with care. It is one clip of one broadcast, labelled by one person. Every number here is an indication, not a benchmark. The next step is the same pipeline on footage the company owns, with questions limited to what is printed or laid out on screen.

Method. Model: Cloudflare Clef via the OpenRouter Decisions API, question schema version 4. Frames sampled every 5 s from a 330 s clip. Answers are the highest-probability option; confidence is that probability. An event fires when a new answer holds for two consecutive frames at 60% confidence or more. Comparisons used Clef-Flash, one-per-second sampling around each event, and previous-frame context.
